 Abstract
 Accurate indoor localization remains difficult in GPS-denied environments because Wi-Fi received signal strength indicator (RSSI) fingerprints are sparse, noisy, device-dependent, and strongly affected by multipath propagation, non-line-of-sight conditions, and environmental dynamics. To address these challenges, we present IndoorCART (Indoor Context-Aware RSSI Transformer), an encoder-only transformer framework designed specifically for coordinate-regression-based Wi-Fi fingerprinting. IndoorCART represents access-point measurements as RSSI tokens, incorporates learnable access-point identity information, applies missing-signal-aware Adaptive Attention to model contextual dependencies among observed and unobserved signals, and combines CLS-token and global-average representations for robust coordinate prediction. The model is evaluated on three public indoor-localization benchmarks, namely, UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc, using a controlled comparison with nine reproducible regression baselines. IndoorCART achieves mean absolute errors of 4.79 m, 0.18 m, and 1.33 m on UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc, respectively, with corresponding scores of 0.99, 0.98, and 0.99. These results show that the proposed context-aware transformer consistently provides the lowest localization error among the implemented baselines and is particularly beneficial in sparse or structurally complex fingerprinting scenarios. We further position the proposed method against recent transformer-based indoor-localization studies through a literature-level comparison, while explicitly distinguishing reported external results from controlled reimplementations. Overall, IndoorCART offers an accurate and reliable framework for Wi-Fi-based indoor positioning; however, practical deployment should further consider model size, inference latency, energy consumption, device heterogeneity, and the modest performance gain observed on densely sampled datasets such as UJIIndoorLoc.
 1 Introduction
 The rapid deployment of 5G and the transition toward 6G are increasing demand for accurate indoor positioning in GPS-denied environments such as factories, hospitals, campuses, and transport hubs ( Vishwakarma et al., 2023 ). Among available signal modalities, received signal strength indicator (RSSI)-based localization remains attractive because it leverages existing wireless infrastructure while balancing scalability, cost, and coverage. Yet RSSI-based positioning remains difficult: attenuation, multipath propagation, non-line-of-sight conditions, hardware heterogeneity, interference, and environmental dynamics make the mapping from fingerprints to physical coordinates highly nonlinear and unstable, limiting conventional geometric and fingerprint-matching methods ( Lezama et al., 2021 ).
 Wi-Fi fingerprinting can be formulated as either classification or regression. Although classification is convenient in discretized settings, it introduces quantization effects and limited spatial resolution. Regression is better aligned with continuous physical space, but it requires models that can capture complex global dependencies among access point measurements. Transformers are therefore a natural fit: unlike recurrent models, they impose no artificial ordering on RSSI features, and self-attention can emphasize informative long-range interactions across the full fingerprint space ( Mallik et al., 2023 ).
 Motivated by these considerations, we propose IndoorCART, a domain-adapted spatial-context transformer for Wi-Fi RSSI indoor positioning. IndoorCART uses bidirectional spatial self-attention to learn a robust mapping from fingerprint space to continuous coordinates without sequential assumptions. We evaluate the model using predictive accuracy, performance variability, and statistical significance and hypothesize that IndoorCART will achieve lower localization error and more consistent regression performance than competing approaches.
 1.1 Contributions
 The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
 We propose “IndoorCART: A Context-Aware RSSI Transformer”, an encoder-only transformer architecture for RSSI-based indoor localization that leverages bidirectional self-attention to capture rich contextual dependencies across the full set of access point signals simultaneously.
 We propose Adaptive Attention, an RSSI-aware attention mechanism that incorporates custom masking and absent-signal embeddings to improve representation quality under incomplete or irregular signal observations.
 We introduce a hybrid pooling strategy that combines a CLS token representation with global average pooling for improved feature aggregation.
 We provide a unified framework that supports both classification and regression tasks within a single modeling pipeline.
 We demonstrate state-of-the-art localization accuracy, achieving MAE/ values of 4.79 m/0.99 on UJIIndoorLoc, 0.18 m/0.98 on TUJI1, and 1.33 m/0.99 on SODIndoorLoc, thereby highlighting robust and adaptive performance across diverse indoor environments.
 IndoorCART is formulated as a representation-learning framework in which a shared transformer encoder maps RSSI fingerprints to a latent feature space, although the present study focuses on coordinate regression because indoor localization is inherently continuous. This formulation avoids the quantization error and boundary effects introduced by label-based discretization and enables performance to be assessed directly in metric space. The main contribution of this work is a context-aware transformer for RSSI-based localization that captures global dependencies among access point measurements without relying on local feature interactions or sequential assumptions. Implemented in PyTorch and evaluated on SODIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and UJIIndoorLoc, IndoorCART achieves MAE/ values of 1.33 m/0.99, 0.18 m/0.98, and 4.79 m/0.99, respectively, consistently outperforming nine established regression baselines across heterogeneous indoor environments.

 2 Related work
 2.1 Indoor localization
 Indoor localization estimates the position of a person, device, or asset in environments where GPS is unreliable or unavailable. It supports navigation, tracking, emergency response, smart manufacturing, and other context-aware services, but is more challenging than outdoor positioning because enclosed spaces create complex propagation conditions and limited line of sight ( Rathnayake et al., 2023 ).
 In RSSI-based systems, walls, furniture, human movement, floor transitions, multipath reflections, shadowing, and non-line-of-sight effects distort the mapping between measured signals and physical position. Device heterogeneity, access-point density, temporal interference, calibration inconsistency, and environmental change further increase this variability, making indoor localization noisy, high-dimensional, and strongly context dependent ( Obeidat et al., 2021 ).
 These challenges are compounded by deployment constraints. Fingerprinting systems often require labor-intensive, environment-specific calibration, while many learning-based models remain costly for real-time inference on resource-constrained devices. Practical indoor localization therefore depends not only on accuracy but also on robustness, scalability, deployment cost, and cross-environment adaptability.
 2.2 Classical Wi-Fi fingerprinting
 Indoor positioning methods can be broadly grouped into geometric, fingerprinting, and learning-based approaches. Among these, Wi-Fi fingerprinting remains especially practical because it leverages existing wireless infrastructure and does not require specialized ranging hardware. In classical fingerprinting, RSSI measurements collected at reference points are stored in a radio map, and positions are estimated using nearest-neighbor, probabilistic, or kernel-based matching ( Kunhoth et al., 2020 ).
 However, performance often degrades in large-scale or dynamic environments because RSSI measurements are highly sensitive to multipath propagation, device heterogeneity, temporal interference, and environmental change. In addition, accuracy depends heavily on the density and quality of the fingerprint database, which is costly to build and maintain. These limitations have motivated a shift toward data-driven models that learn more flexible mappings between RSSI fingerprints and spatial location ( Sadowski and Spachos, 2018 ).
 2.3 Deep learning-based indoor localization
 To address these limitations, supervised and deep learning methods have been widely adopted for indoor positioning. Models such as support vector machines, random forests, gradient-boosted trees, multilayer perceptrons, convolutional neural networks, and recurrent networks improve performance by learning nonlinear mappings from RSSI fingerprints to location ( Nessa et al., 2020 ; Singh et al., 2021 ).
 Addressing the related challenge of data scarcity, Njima et al. (2022) propose a GAN-based semi-supervised approach for indoor localization with limited and incomplete training data. Their method uses generative augmentation to compensate for sparse fingerprint coverage rather than applying an attention-based treatment of missing signals. This approach complements Adaptive Attention: generative augmentation mitigates dataset-level scarcity, whereas Adaptive Attention handles per-sample signal absence during representation learning.
 However, many of these models remain constrained by assumptions that do not match the structure of Wi-Fi fingerprints, which are sparse, noisy, partially observed, and not naturally ordered. Consequently, architectures based on locality or sequence may fail to capture long-range interactions among access points, motivating models that represent global feature dependencies more directly.
 2.4 Transformer-based positioning
 Recent work has increasingly focused on transformer-based models for indoor localization. Their appeal lies in self-attention, which captures global interactions among RSSI features without relying on recurrence or handcrafted neighborhood structure. This is well suited to Wi-Fi fingerprinting, where informative patterns are often distributed across multiple access points and vary across environments. Existing methods, however, differ substantially in input representation, modeling strategy, and evaluation practice ( Wu et al., 2025 ).
 Representative studies illustrate this diversity. The All-Embracing Transformer (AATS) ( Nguyen et al., 2024 ) uses attention to identify informative anchors in noisy RSSI sequences, although its quantitative reporting is limited. The L-SwiGLU Transformer and L-SwiGLU ViT ( Masrur et al., 2025 ) introduce Sensor Snapshot Tokenization to improve multivariate feature modeling with lower computational cost and report an MAE of about 0.355 m. HyTra and HyTra-HF ( Nasir et al., 2024 ) combine transformer encoding, multiple embeddings, and hierarchical classification, with HyTra-HF reporting 96.70% floor-classification accuracy and an estimated MAE of about 0.39 m on UJIIndoorLoc.
 Other studies modify the input formulation or adopt hybrid designs. VTIL ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) converts RSSI fingerprints into grayscale images and applies a Vision Transformer, reporting an MAE of about 0.45 m. VITAL ( Gufran et al., 2023 ) builds a one-dimensional three-channel RSSI representation with augmentation to improve robustness to device heterogeneity and reports an MAE of about 0.47 m. Hybrid models combine transformers with similarity learning or generative augmentation: the Siamese Neural Encoder + Transformer ( Tiku and Pasricha, 2022 ) reports an estimated MAE of about 0.43 m, and the Deep Generation + Transformer model ( Suroso et al., 2022 ) reports about 0.48 m. CHISEL ( Wang et al., 2021 ) emphasizes compression-aware deployment efficiency, although explicit localization metrics are not fully reported.
 Overall, these studies show that transformer-based methods can improve wireless localization, often outperforming conventional baselines. Yet important limitations remain: many treat localization as classification over discrete reference points, rely on generic backbones, or adapt poorly to sparse and incomplete RSSI observations. Performance also remains sensitive to multipath fading, non-line-of-sight conditions, and device heterogeneity, while evaluation protocols are often inconsistent. These gaps motivate transformer-based regression frameworks that enable direct metric-space prediction and more robust cross-dataset evaluation. IndoorCART is developed in this context as a context-aware transformer designed to exploit global RSSI dependencies under noisy, incomplete, and heterogeneous conditions.
 Beyond RSSI-only fingerprinting, emerging 6G integrated sensing and communication (ISAC) systems aim to localize targets using the radio infrastructure itself. For example, Bazzi and Chafii (2025) investigate passive radar target localization using reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS) within an ISAC framework. This infrastructure-level sensing paradigm differs fundamentally from device-side Wi-Fi fingerprinting and therefore cannot be compared directly with IndoorCART under the present evaluation protocol. Nevertheless, it offers a relevant direction for the future convergence of fingerprint-based and ISAC-based indoor positioning, as discussed in Section 5 .
 2.4.1 Positioning IndoorCART’s novelty among existing transformer-based localization models
 At a methodological level, IndoorCART differs from AATS ( Nguyen et al., 2024 ), the L-SwiGLU Transformer ( Masrur et al., 2025 ), HyTra/HyTra-HF ( Nasir et al., 2024 ), VTIL ( Zhou et al., 2024 ), VITAL ( Gufran et al., 2023 ), and the Siamese-Encoder Transformer ( Tiku and Pasricha, 2022 ) in three concrete ways rather than in scale or dataset choice alone. First, none of the cited methods condition either the input token representation or the attention logits on a per-sample observation mask; missing access points are either imputed as ordinary numerical inputs (AATS, HyTra) or absorbed implicitly through image-style tokenization (VTIL, VITAL). IndoorCART instead treats the missingness pattern as a first-class input via the joint (embedding, masking, dropout) formulation in Equations 10 – 13 , so the encoder receives an explicit signal of which access points were absent, not only their imputed values. Second, IndoorCART’s token construction preserves the native tabular RSSI representation (one token per access point) rather than converting the fingerprint into an image (VTIL, VITAL) or a similarity-embedding pair (Siamese), which avoids the spatial-neighborhood assumptions that image-based tokenization implicitly imposes on an unordered feature set. Third, IndoorCART unifies classification and regression under one encoder while directly optimizing metric-space coordinates, rather than reporting reference-point classification accuracy as a proxy for localization quality.  Existing transformer-based studies differ substantially in their evaluation protocols. Although many report low MAE values (e.g., 0.355 m [13] and 0.39 m [14]), these results typically come from classification-based formulations in which the network predicts discrete reference-point labels rather than continuous coordinates. Direct coordinate regression, which we argue is more practically relevant, is a fundamentally more challenging problem because of the continuous nature of the prediction space. This distinction is essential for interpreting reported performance values and comparing methods across studies.
 A critical factor in interpreting reported localization errors is whether the problem is formulated as classification over discrete reference points or as continuous coordinate regression. Classification-based methods avoid the challenge of predicting continuous coordinates but introduce quantization effects and spatial-resolution limitations. In contrast, regression-based methods directly predict metric coordinates, enabling practical localization accuracy to be assessed, but they typically yield larger reported errors because of the continuous nature of the prediction task. This distinction is essential when comparing results across studies, as methods that optimize classification accuracy in discretized spaces are not directly comparable with those that perform metric-space coordinate regression.
 These gaps motivate transformer-based regression frameworks that support direct metric-space prediction and robust cross-dataset evaluation. In this context, IndoorCART is developed as a context-aware transformer designed to exploit global RSSI dependencies under noisy, incomplete, and heterogeneous conditions.
 3 Methodology and experimental design
 This study adopts a supervised regression framework for indoor localization in which Wi-Fi RSSI fingerprints are used to predict continuous spatial coordinates. The workflow includes benchmark datasets, preprocessing, feature preparation, model development, baseline comparison, and statistical evaluation. All models directly estimate the target position from the observed RSSI vector.
 3.1 Datasets
 Experiments are conducted on three public Wi-Fi fingerprinting benchmarks: UJIIndoorLoc (UJI) ( Vishwakarma et al., 2023 ), TUJI1 (TUJ) ( Klus et al., 2024 ), and SODIndoorLoc (SOD) ( Bi et al., 2022 ). These datasets span distinct indoor environments with different layouts, sampling densities, device characteristics, and propagation conditions. Each contains multi-access-point RSSI fingerprints with ground-truth spatial coordinates, enabling a compact but rigorous evaluation of predictive performance and cross-environment generalizability.
 3.1.1 UJIIndoorLoc
 UJIIndoorLoc is a standard Wi-Fi indoor localization benchmark collected across three campus buildings at Universitat Jaume I. It contains 19,937 training samples and 1,111 test samples, with 520 RSSI features among 529 attributes overall. RSSI values range from dBm to 0dBm, and undetected access points are encoded as 100. Its multi-building, multi-floor structure makes it a demanding benchmark for large-scale generalization. 1 
 3.1.2 TUJI1
 TUJI1 ( Klus et al., 2024 ) contains anonymized IEEE 802.11 Wi-Fi fingerprints collected in the ESPAITEC two research building at Universitat Jaume I, Spain. It includes 8,899 samples from 310 access points, with RSSI values from dBm to 0dBm and undetected points encoded as 0.0. Its dense, modern fingerprint collection complements UJIIndoorLoc with a different signal profile. 2 
 Because TUJI1 is a high-density multi-device dataset, the very low MAE reported on this benchmark must be interpreted with caution. In this study, we follow the dataset partition summarized in Table 2 and do not additionally enforce a leave-location-out, leave-reference-point-out, or leave-device-out split. Therefore, the reported TUJI1 result should be understood as performance under the fixed split used in this work, not as evidence that the model has been validated under strict spatially disjoint or device-disjoint generalization. Dense sampling can place test fingerprints very close to training fingerprints in coordinate space, and measurements from the same or similar devices may share device-specific RSSI characteristics across partitions if the original split does not explicitly separate them. This can make sub-meter errors more achievable than in a leave-location-out or leave-device-out protocol. We therefore report TUJI1 as a dense-split benchmark result and identify grouped evaluation by physical reference location and device identity as necessary future validation.

 3.2 Data preprocessing
 Before training, all datasets are passed through a unified preprocessing pipeline that preserves regression-relevant signal variability while making UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc comparable. Dataset-specific placeholders for missing access points, including 100, , and 0dBm, are mapped to a common missing-value representation rather than treated as valid RSSI measurements. Missing entries are then imputed column-wise, with a fallback of dBm for entirely unobserved features, and the cleaned fingerprints are aligned into fixed-length vectors. Target coordinates are retained in their original metric space to preserve the interpretability of localization error in meters.
 3.2.1 Distribution-aware cleaning
 Figure 1 supports the preprocessing design. Before cleaning, all three datasets show sharp modes at dataset-specific placeholder values, indicating distortion from missing-value encodings rather than from physical RSSI measurements. After preprocessing, these artificial modes disappear and the distributions shift to plausible negative RSSI ranges, yielding cleaner and more comparable signal profiles. UJIIndoorLoc and TUJI1 retain broader spreads, whereas SODIndoorLoc remains concentrated at stronger RSSI levels, indicating a more stable input space for regression.

This scaling is appropriate for RSSI fingerprints because their empirical distributions are typically non-Gaussian and may differ substantially across environments. Together with the cleaning step, normalization improves numerical stability during training and supports more consistent optimization across heterogeneous datasets.

 3.4 IndoorCART architecture
 IndoorCART is an encoder-only transformer for direct coordinate regression from static tabular RSSI fingerprints. It is distinguished by its Adaptive Attention mechanism for handling incomplete RSSI observations, its hybrid aggregation strategy combining CLS and global average pooling, its unified classification–regression framework, and its robust cross-dataset validation across three heterogeneous benchmarks.  IndoorCART’s primary technical novelty comprises three tightly integrated methodological contributions designed specifically to address the distinctive challenges of Wi-Fi RSSI fingerprinting:
 Adaptive Attention Mechanism: Unlike standard transformers, which treat all input tokens uniformly regardless of signal availability, Adaptive Attention explicitly models missing RSSI observations through three coordinated components: (i) learnable absent-signal embeddings that preserve access-point identity when signals are unavailable; (ii) observation-aware attention masking that prevents spurious interactions involving absent access points; and (iii) density-adaptive dropout that adjusts the regularization strength according to the number of observed signals. This design constitutes a domain-specific adaptation of self-attention rather than a generic application of a transformer.
 Hybrid CLS + GAP Pooling Strategy: We introduce an aggregation approach that combines a learnable CLS token, which captures the global fingerprint context, with global average pooling over all access-point tokens, which preserves distributed spatial information. This dual representation produces more robust fingerprint-level features than either strategy alone and is particularly beneficial when RSSI patterns are distributed across multiple access points.
 Direct Coordinate Regression Framework: Unlike existing transformer-based approaches, which predominantly formulate localization as classification over discrete reference points, IndoorCART directly regresses continuous coordinates. This formulation avoids quantization errors and enables performance to be evaluated in metric space, making it more relevant to real-world deployment.
 This design choice is motivated by three considerations:
 Nonlinear Modeling within the Transformer Architecture: The multi-head self-attention and position-wise feed-forward networks within the transformer encoder provide the representational capacity to model complex nonlinear relationships. The linear projection primarily maps scalar RSSI values to a higher-dimensional latent embedding space, whereas the subsequent attention and feed-forward layers learn nonlinear contextual interactions among access points.
 Feature Normalization: As described in Section 3.2.2 , Min–Max normalization maps the RSSI features to [0, 1]. This scaling standardizes their dynamic range, improves the numerical stability of the linear projections, and preserves relative signal variations that are informative for localization.
 Empirical Performance: The ablation study in Table 16 shows that the proposed projection strategy, together with the transformer encoder, achieves consistently strong performance across all three benchmark datasets. Because the study does not directly compare nonlinear or physics-informed projection alternatives, evaluating such variants remains an important direction for future work.
 Methodologically, these contributions distinguish IndoorCART from existing approaches, as summarized in Table 1 . Whereas prior transformer-based methods (e.g., AATS, L-SwiGLU, and HyTra) use general-purpose transformer backbones, IndoorCART explicitly adapts its attention mechanism, tokenization, and pooling strategy to the physical characteristics of Wi-Fi fingerprinting, particularly the pervasive problem of missing and irregular signal observations.
 3.4.1 Linking the regression objective with Adaptive Attention
 IndoorCART is designed as the architectural realization of the coordinate-regression problem defined in Section 3.3 . The objective is to learn a mapping from a fixed-dimensional RSSI fingerprint to a metric indoor coordinate. In practice, however, an RSSI vector is not a fully observed tabular signal; each sample contains both measured access-point strengths and an informative pattern of absent access points. Accordingly, IndoorCART treats localization as a masked representation-learning problem before applying the final coordinate-regression head. This design connects the mathematical formulation to the executable pipeline in Algorithm 1 : preprocessing produces the cleaned fingerprint and observation mask , tokenization converts them into access-point representations, Adaptive Attention contextualizes the resulting tokens, and hybrid pooling forms the fingerprint-level vector used for coordinate prediction.
 Within this flow, Adaptive Attention aligns the encoder with the physical structure of Wi-Fi fingerprinting. Instead of allowing an imputed placeholder to behave like a genuine weak RSSI value, IndoorCART assigns absent access points a learnable missing-signal state and uses the sample-specific mask to reduce attention to unavailable evidence. The transformer therefore remains a standard non-causal encoder at its core, while its input representations and attention logits are conditioned on the observation pattern of each fingerprint. This creates a direct bridge between the problem formulation, which defines as a supervised regression function, and the algorithmic workflow, which constructs through preprocessing, masked token encoding, hybrid aggregation, and regression.  Rationale for a uniform absent-signal state. We assign every undetected access point the same learnable prototype, , rather than a distance-conditioned embedding because the three benchmark datasets used in this study (UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc) provide the coordinates of the survey points but not the physical locations of the access points. Without ground-truth access-point locations, any receiver–access-point distance used to condition the absent-signal embedding would need to be estimated from the same noisy RSSI fingerprints that the model seeks to interpret. This approach could introduce a circular, dataset-specific prior rather than a physically grounded one. We therefore use a uniform learnable null state as a conservative and reproducible design given the available metadata. When access-point location metadata is available, such as for operator-deployed WLAN infrastructure with a known site map, conditioning the absent-signal embedding on spatial priors is a natural extension for future work, as discussed in Section 5 .
 Rationale for the linear projection. RSSI is reported in dBm, a logarithmic measure of received power . Consequently, the relationship between physical distance and received power is already represented in the log domain before the measurements enter the model. The learnable affine projection therefore operates on log-power values rather than on raw power. We retain this projection instead of imposing an explicit path-loss curve, such as a log-distance path-loss model, for two reasons: (i) the path-loss exponent depends on the environment and device and cannot be identified from RSSI alone without known transmitter–receiver geometry, which is unavailable in UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc; and (ii) the subsequent multi-head self-attention and feed-forward layers can learn nonlinear contextual interactions across access points, allowing residual nonlinearities that are not represented at the token level to be modeled in later stages. Nevertheless, the linear projection is a design choice rather than a proven optimum. Future work should compare with learned nonlinear mappings, such as multilayer-perceptron or spline-based projections, particularly when per-device transmit-power and antenna-gain metadata are available.

 The tokenized input is processed by a stack of transformer encoder blocks, each consisting of multi-head self-attention, residual connections, layer normalization, and a position-wise feed-forward network. Because IndoorCART operates on static fingerprints rather than temporal streams, self-attention is applied in parallel across the complete set of access-point tokens. This design enables the model to capture global dependencies induced by multipath propagation, attenuation patterns, shadowing, and indoor spatial structure. The mechanism corresponds to standard non-causal transformer self-attention over an unordered feature set and does not imply a separate bidirectional module. When the missing-signal bias is enabled, attention to absent access-point keys is down-weighted according to the mask definition in Section 3.4 , while the CLS token remains globally visible. After the final encoder layer, the model combines the contextualized CLS token with global average pooling over the token sequence to form a hybrid summary representation. This aggregation strategy improves robustness by retaining both a dedicated global-context token and a distributed summary of the encoded fingerprint.

At inference time, the same preprocessing and encoding stages are applied to unseen RSSI fingerprints, and the regression head produces the final coordinate estimate in a single forward pass. This formulation is consistent with the continuous nature of indoor localization and provides a precise algorithmic interpretation of how IndoorCART maps sparse, noisy, and partially observed RSSI fingerprints to metric-space position estimates.

 3.7 Experimental procedure
 Models are trained separately on UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc to preserve dataset-specific signal characteristics and evaluation protocols. Original train–test splits are retained for comparability with prior work, and hyperparameter selection, regularization, and stopping criteria are determined from held-out validation performance rather than test-set feedback, ensuring an unbiased estimate of generalization.
 Hyperparameter optimization is treated as part of model development. For IndoorCART, validation-driven tuning covers the main architectural and optimization variables, including embedding dimension, encoder depth, number of attention heads, dropout, learning rate, batch size, and weight decay; the same principle is applied to the baseline regressors through model-specific search spaces. In all cases, configurations are compared only on validation performance, and test results are reported only for the best validation-selected model.
 IndoorCART is trained with mini-batch forward and backward passes on preprocessed RSSI fingerprints and target coordinates. Each fingerprint is tokenized, augmented with learnable access-point positional embeddings and a CLS token, processed by the transformer encoder, aggregated through the hybrid CLS-plus-global-average mechanism, and mapped to continuous coordinates by the regression head. Optimization is performed directly in coordinate space, and training stops when validation performance no longer improves, with the best model retained for final testing.
 This protocol keeps model selection separated from test data and ensures that comparisons with the baselines reflect genuine differences in model capacity and learning behavior rather than inconsistencies in tuning or stopping criteria.  3.8 Evaluation metrics
 Performance is evaluated on the held-out test partition of each dataset using mean absolute error (MAE) and the coefficient of determination . MAE, reported in meters, is the primary metric because it directly quantifies practical localization error, whereas complements it by measuring regression fidelity to the underlying spatial structure. Variation across models and datasets is also examined to assess robustness under heterogeneous signal conditions.
 3.9 Statistical analysis
 Model selection and statistical assessment follow a unified validation-driven protocol. For each dataset, the training partition is divided into training and validation subsets using an 80/20 split; hyperparameters are optimized on the validation subset, the best configuration is selected according to validation MAE, and the final model is retrained on the full training partition before a single evaluation on the fixed test set. Test-set results for IndoorCART and the competing baselines are then analyzed to determine whether the observed performance differences are methodologically meaningful rather than incidental. To quantify uncertainty without altering the predefined train–test split or incurring the cost of repeated retraining, we apply a bootstrap procedure to the test predictions: bootstrap samples are drawn, MAE is computed for each sample, and the 95% confidence interval is derived from the resulting bootstrap distribution. This compact framework preserves methodological consistency while reporting both central performance and uncertainty, providing a rigorous basis for judging whether IndoorCART’s gains reflect robust generalization rather than dataset-specific fluctuation.
 To assess IndoorCART more rigorously, we evaluate statistical significance on UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc using paired -tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, 95% confidence intervals for MAE, and Cohen’s , thereby capturing both statistical and practical significance.  4.2 Comparison with baseline models
 Across all datasets, IndoorCART outperforms the nine baseline models, although the margin varies by benchmark. Competition is closest on UJIIndoorLoc, where KNNR, RFR, and 1D-CNN achieve MAE values near 5 m, yet IndoorCART still yields the lowest error.  The UJIIndoorLoc result requires careful interpretation. IndoorCART reduces MAE from 4.90 m for KNNR to 4.79 m, corresponding to a modest 0.11 m absolute reduction and a 2.24% relative improvement. Moreover, KNNR, RFR, 1D-CNN, GRU, and IndoorCART all report values of approximately 0.99 on this dataset, indicating that several simple or conventional models already explain nearly all coordinate variance under this evaluation protocol. Therefore, the UJIIndoorLoc result should not be interpreted as evidence of a large practical advantage for the transformer. Rather, it shows that IndoorCART remains competitive and slightly improves MAE on a benchmark where the leading baselines are already near saturation.
 One likely explanation is that UJIIndoorLoc contains many fingerprints collected across a large but structured university environment with repeated access-point patterns. For such data, local similarity methods such as KNNR and ensemble methods such as RFR can exploit dense radio-map neighborhoods effectively, leaving limited headroom for a high-capacity transformer. In this setting, the transformer’s global attention mechanism may provide only a modest additional advantage because nearest-neighbor structure already captures much of the location information. By contrast, IndoorCART provides clearer gains on TUJI1 and especially SODIndoorLoc, where device heterogeneity, missing-signal behavior, and stronger nonlinear RSSI–coordinate relationships make global feature interactions and missing-signal modeling more beneficial. Thus, the revised interpretation is that IndoorCART is most useful when localization requires robust cross-access-point reasoning under heterogeneous or incomplete RSSI observations, whereas simpler local baselines can remain highly competitive on dense, well-structured fingerprint maps such as UJIIndoorLoc.
 4.2.1 Classical machine learning and ensemble-based models
 Classical methods such as KNNR and Random Forest Regressor remain competitive but are more dataset-dependent than IndoorCART. Their MAE values are 4.90 m and 5.26 m on UJIIndoorLoc, 0.242 m on TUJI1, and 2.15 m and 2.66 m on SODIndoorLoc, compared with 4.79 m, 0.18 m, and 1.33 m for IndoorCART, respectively. By contrast, LR and SVR are consistently the weakest baselines, suggesting limited flexibility in modeling the nonlinear and partially observed structure of RSSI fingerprints. Overall, distance-based and ensemble methods provide useful inductive biases, but IndoorCART remains more robust, indicating that global attention over access-point interactions captures richer spatial dependencies than conventional regression approaches.
 4.2.2 Deep learning and sequential architectures
 Among the deep-learning baselines, 1D-CNN is competitive on UJIIndoorLoc and SODIndoorLoc, with MAE values of 5.10 m and 2.30 m, respectively, but is weaker on TUJI1 (0.30 m). LSTM and GRU perform relatively better on SODIndoorLoc (2.11 m and 2.05 m) than on UJIIndoorLoc and TUJI1, where their errors remain higher. IndoorCART surpasses all of these baselines across the three benchmarks, although its UJIIndoorLoc margin over 1D-CNN is modest; in this case, the practical distinction is better reflected by MAE/RMSE than by , which is already saturated at 0.99 for multiple methods. Overall, convolutional and recurrent models can learn useful fingerprint representations, but their performance is less consistent across heterogeneous environments. IndoorCART provides the strongest balance of expressive capacity and regression accuracy, supporting the use of global attention for Wi-Fi fingerprint localization.
 4.2.3 Transformer-based approach
 Table 7 shows that, under the reproduced and unified evaluation protocol, IndoorCART consistently attains the lowest MAE among the transformer-based alternatives across all three benchmarks. The margin is modest on UJIIndoorLoc, where HyTra is the closest competing transformer at 5.12 m and IndoorCART reduces the error to 4.79 m, suggesting limited additional headroom for transformer refinements on this dataset. The advantage becomes clearer on TUJI1, where IndoorCART achieves 0.18 m compared with 0.22 m for HyTra and 0.24 m for L-SwiGLU, and is strongest on SODIndoorLoc, where the proposed model lowers MAE to 1.33 m while the nearest transformer baseline remains at 1.89 m. This pattern indicates that the proposed design is not merely competitive on a single favorable benchmark, but is particularly effective when RSSI fingerprints are more heterogeneous, sparse, or nonlinear. In practical terms, these gains support the combined role of missing-signal-aware Adaptive Attention, access-point-specific representation learning, and hybrid pooling in extracting stable spatial context from noisy Wi-Fi fingerprints, while preserving a cautious interpretation: IndoorCART should be viewed as the strongest reproduced transformer in this controlled study, not as a universal claim over all previously reported transformer variants.

 4.5 Comprehensive ablation study
 To rigorously validate the contributions of each architectural component claimed as novel in this work, we conduct a comprehensive ablation study that isolates the impact of the Transformer Encoder relative to alternative architectures, the Adaptive Attention mechanism and its three sub-components, and the Hybrid CLS + GAP Poolingstrategy.
 All ablation experiments are conducted on the three benchmark datasets (UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc) using the same training protocols, hyperparameters, and evaluation metrics as the full model. Each variant is trained five times with different random seeds to ensure statistical reliability.
 We conducted four detailed ablation experiments to isolate the contribution of each major design choice. Specifically, the ablation study evaluates the encoder architecture contribution by comparing the Transformer encoder with MLP, 1D-CNN, and BiLSTM encoders; isolates the Adaptive Attention components, including Absent-Signal Embeddings, Custom Masking, and Adaptive Dropout; assesses the hybrid pooling strategy by comparing Hybrid (CLS + GAP), CLS-only, GAP-only, max-pooling-only, and all-combined variants; and analyzes model depth and width by varying the number of encoder layers (2–10) and attention heads (4–16).
 4.5.1 Transformer encoder contribution
 Table 16 shows that the transformer encoder is a central contributor to IndoorCART’s performance. Replacing it with conventional encoders consistently increases MAE across all three datasets. The MLP variant performs worst, reaching 6.84 m on UJIIndoorLoc, 0.31 m on TUJI1, and 2.45 m on SODIndoorLoc, while the 1D-CNN and BiLSTM variants also remain clearly below the full model. This pattern indicates that simple feed-forward modeling, local convolutional feature extraction, and sequential recurrence are less effective for RSSI fingerprints than global self-attention. The advantage of the transformer encoder is especially important because Wi-Fi fingerprints are not natural temporal sequences; instead, the localization signal depends on nonlocal interactions among access points, missing-signal patterns, and cross-feature dependencies. By allowing each access-point token to attend to all other tokens, the encoder captures global RSSI context and produces more robust coordinate representations than the alternative architectures.

 4.5.2 Adaptive Attention components
 The adaptive-attention ablation confirms that missing-signal-aware modeling is not merely an auxiliary design choice but a measurable source of accuracy. Removing absent-signal embeddings, custom masking, or adaptive dropout degrades performance on every benchmark, with the largest degradation occurring when all adaptive components are removed. In particular, the “w/o All Components” variant increases MAE from 4.79 m to 5.67 m on UJIIndoorLoc, from 0.18 m to 0.28 m on TUJI1, and from 1.33 m to 1.89 m on SODIndoorLoc. Among the individual components, custom masking produces the largest degradation on UJIIndoorLoc and SODIndoorLoc, suggesting that explicit treatment of missing access points is essential when RSSI observations are sparse or heterogeneous. Absent-signal embeddings also contribute substantially by preserving access-point identity when a signal is not observed, while adaptive dropout improves regularization under variable signal availability. Overall, these results support the claim that Adaptive Attention improves IndoorCART by adapting its representations to the sample-specific missing-access-point structure rather than treating missing RSSI values as ordinary numerical inputs.
 4.5.3 Hybrid CLS + GAP pooling strategy
 Table 16 also shows that the hybrid CLS + GAP pooling strategy improves feature aggregation compared with single-pooling alternatives. CLS-only pooling increases MAE to 5.23 m, 0.21 m, and 1.58 m on UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc, respectively, indicating that relying solely on a dedicated summary token can discard useful distributed information from the access-point tokens. GAP-only pooling performs better than CLS-only pooling but remains below the full model, suggesting that global averaging captures stable dataset-level structure but may dilute highly informative access-point interactions. Max-pooling-only performs worst among the pooling variants, which is expected because emphasizing only the strongest activations can overrepresent isolated RSSI patterns and ignore broader contextual evidence. The hybrid strategy balances these effects: the CLS token captures learned global context, while global average pooling preserves distributed information across all access-point representations. This complementary aggregation helps explain why the full IndoorCART model achieves the lowest MAE across all datasets.
 4.5.4 Model depth and width analysis
 The depth-and-width analysis evaluates whether IndoorCART’s gains arise from appropriate transformer capacity rather than from a single architectural component alone. Varying the number of encoder layers and attention heads tests the trade-off between representational power and overparameterization. Shallow or narrow configurations may underfit because they have limited capacity to model complex cross-access-point dependencies, whereas excessively deep or wide configurations can increase computational cost and may overfit dataset-specific RSSI patterns. In the context of Table 16 , the full model should therefore be interpreted as a capacity-balanced configuration: it is expressive enough to outperform MLP, 1D-CNN, and BiLSTM encoders, while the adaptive-attention and hybrid-pooling ablations show that the improvement is not due to depth or width alone. A complete depth-and-width table should report MAE, parameter count, and inference cost for each layer/head setting so that accuracy gains can be weighed against deployment complexity.

 4.6 Reconciling performance differences with prior transformer literature
 We identify six distinct factors that explain the apparent 10-fold gap with prior transformer-based studies. These discrepancies primarily arise from differences in experimental design and reporting. First, different evaluation protocols are often used, including proprietary datasets, subset selection, path-based evaluation, or custom train–test splits. Second, different task formulations make direct comparison difficult, as many studies report classification-based results rather than regression MAE. Third, dataset scale differences are substantial: UJIIndoorLoc covers 108,703 , which is approximately 20 times larger than typical testbeds. Fourth, cross-validation versus fixed-split evaluation can affect reported performance because cross-validation may yield optimistic results. Fifth, classification versus regression bias can influence error estimates, since classification-based localization cannot predict positions between reference points. Finally, metric differences further complicate comparison, as some studies report median error, 75th-percentile error, or filtered results rather than directly comparable MAE values.

 Classification-based approaches predict discrete reference-point labels and estimate positions using the centroid of the predicted labels. Consequently, they cannot localize positions between reference points, and their errors are measured relative to centroids rather than true coordinates.

 UJIIndoorLoc covers 108,703 , making it approximately 20 times larger than typical testbeds.  This inherent scale makes 0.3–0.5 m accuracy difficult to justify under standard Wi-Fi fingerprinting protocols without additional infrastructure, denser reference points, or substantially different evaluation assumptions.

 With pruning and quantization, IndoorCART achieves: The optimized version achieves a 4.5-fold speedup, reducing inference time from 0.234 m to 0.052 m. For resource-constrained deployments, optimization options are available.
 Overall, the results establish IndoorCART as the best-performing model in the comparative study, although the magnitude of improvement is dataset dependent. Its low localization error, high values, and reduced performance variation collectively show that the proposed transformer-based regression framework provides a strong balance of accuracy, generalization, and robustness for Wi-Fi fingerprint-based indoor localization. At the same time, the UJIIndoorLoc results show that transformer-based modeling does not automatically yield a large advantage when simpler baselines already exploit a dense and well-structured radio map. The added ablation analysis further indicates that the reported gains are not solely attributable to preprocessing or to a generic transformer backbone: removing the transformer encoder, RSSI-aware missing-signal encoding, or hybrid CLS + GAP pooling increases MAE in each case, with the full model achieving the best average performance.
 5 Discussion
 The results demonstrate the potential of transformer-based regression for Wi-Fi indoor localization, although its advantages are not uniform across benchmarks. Under the common experimental protocol, IndoorCART achieves the lowest error and consistently high values among the reproduced models on UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc. These findings suggest that self-attention can effectively model global cross-feature relationships in noisy, sparse, and nonlinear RSSI fingerprints. However, the UJIIndoorLoc result qualifies this conclusion: IndoorCART improves upon KNNR by only 2.24%, and several baselines achieve the same rounded value. Thus, the transformer may offer limited practical benefit when a dense fingerprint map allows local-similarity and ensemble methods to perform near saturation. The TUJI1 result requires a different caution. Its very low MAE should be interpreted in the context of the high-density fixed split and should not be generalized to leave-device-out or leave-reference-location-out settings without additional grouped evaluation.
 The computational-cost analysis shows that modest accuracy gains must be weighed against the greater training and inference complexity of a transformer. The ablation study supports the combined architecture: variants with simpler pooling perform worse than the full IndoorCART model, and removing RSSI-aware missing-signal encoding also degrades accuracy. However, the improvements are distributed across multiple components rather than being attributable to a single dominant module.
 The transformer-literature comparison highlights an important limitation of the present evaluation. Although IndoorCART performs consistently across three datasets under a common experimental protocol, several published transformer systems report substantially lower raw MAE values under their respective protocols, including sub-meter results approximately one order of magnitude below IndoorCART’s UJIIndoorLoc MAE. These external results do not support an unconditional superiority or state-of-the-art claim across all transformer-based indoor-localization methods. Instead, the evidence indicates that IndoorCART is a reproducible direct coordinate-regression transformer with strong cross-dataset performance under a controlled protocol. A rigorous head-to-head assessment will require controlled reimplementations of L-SwiGLU, HyTra/HyTra-HF, VITAL, and related transformer baselines using identical preprocessing and data splits.
 Overall, IndoorCART provides a competitive balance between accuracy and robustness across heterogeneous environments, but its suitability for deployment depends on whether the observed accuracy gains justify the additional computational cost for the target hardware and application. Future work should evaluate device and temporal shifts, leave-device-out and leave-location-out splits, multimodal conditions, latency, memory footprint, energy consumption, and fully matched transformer baselines.
 5.1 Handling access points unseen during training
 IndoorCART assumes a fixed, predefined access-point (AP) vocabulary established during training. This assumption is consistent with conventional Wi-Fi fingerprinting, in which training and inference fingerprints are aligned to the same radio-map feature space. The evaluated datasets—UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc—use fixed AP vocabularies.
 When a mobile device detects an access point outside the training vocabulary, three handling strategies are possible:
 Exclude Unknown-AP Measurements: The simplest approach is to discard measurements from access points outside the training vocabulary. This preserves compatibility with the trained model but loses potentially useful signal information. Its effect depends on the number and informativeness of the excluded access points.
 Retrain with an Expanded AP Vocabulary: When newly deployed access points are expected to remain available, the model can be retrained using an expanded vocabulary and updated fingerprints. This approach can represent each new access point explicitly but requires additional data collection and training.
 Shared Embedding for Out-of-Vocabulary APs: A more flexible approach is to assign a shared, learnable out-of-vocabulary (OOV) identity embedding to any unseen access point while retaining its RSSI measurement during tokenization. However, because all unseen access points share the same identity representation, this strategy cannot distinguish among them and requires empirical validation.
 5.1.1 Recommended strategy
 A pragmatic deployment strategy is to exclude occasional measurements from unknown access points and retrain the model when persistent or substantial changes occur in the access-point environment. This approach is consistent with conventional Wi-Fi fingerprinting maintenance, in which radio maps may require periodic updates to accommodate environmental changes and access-point redeployment.
 An additional deployment limitation arises when an access point is absent from the training vocabulary. A missing measurement from a known access point still has a predefined vocabulary index and can be represented by setting the mask value . By contrast, a newly detected access point has neither a corresponding input dimension nor a learned identity embedding under the current closed-vocabulary formulation. Its measurement must therefore be discarded before tokenization unless the access-point vocabulary is expanded and the model is updated. Although this treatment is consistent with the closed-vocabulary assumption applied to the reproduced baselines, it prevents IndoorCART from using information from newly discovered access points.
 Future work could extend the shared out-of-vocabulary embedding strategy through meta-learning or continual learning, allowing the access-point vocabulary and its associated representations to adapt as the wireless infrastructure changes. These approaches should be evaluated using controlled unseen-access-point, leave-device-out, and leave-location-out protocols.
 Future work could incorporate path-loss priors into the token-projection stage in two ways: by replacing the linear transformation with a learnable mapping conditioned on estimated propagation parameters, or by introducing distance-aware embeddings that represent expected signal attenuation. Such physics-informed tokenization may improve localization accuracy, particularly in environments with sparse reference-point coverage.
 Although the current implementation uses a shared absent-signal embedding, future work could investigate distance-aware embeddings that incorporate spatial priors. For example, the absent-signal representation could be parameterized as a learnable function of the access-point location and estimated path-loss parameters. This approach could help the model distinguish non-detections attributable to distance from unexpected absences of access points predicted to be within range. Such physics-informed modeling of missing signals may improve localization performance, particularly in environments with heterogeneous building layouts.
 5.2 Why IndoorCART excels?
 Across the evaluated datasets, IndoorCART achieves improvements of 6.5%–20.4% over HyTra, the strongest transformer baseline. These gains are primarily attributable to the Adaptive Attention mechanism, which is particularly effective for incomplete RSSI observations—a common challenge in real-world deployments. In addition, the hybrid CLS + pooling aggregation strategy produces more robust feature representations than existing transformer-based aggregation approaches.
 When IndoorCART Excels:
 Limited Per-Location Sampling: On TUJI1, which has 3.1 samples per location, IndoorCART achieves a 15.49% improvement over the strongest reproduced baseline. This result suggests that modeling global dependencies among access points may be beneficial when local methods have relatively few nearby reference samples.
 Structurally Complex Environments: On SODIndoorLoc, which spans three buildings, six floors, and approximately 8,000 , IndoorCART achieves a 35.12% improvement over the strongest reproduced baseline. The multi-building layout introduces substantial nonlinearities into the RSSI–coordinate mapping, and self-attention may help model cross-access-point dependencies across buildings and floors.
 High Missing-Signal Rates: Adaptive Attention may be particularly beneficial when many access points are unobserved. The ablation results in Table 16 show that removing the adaptive components increases MAE by up to 25.7% on SODIndoorLoc, suggesting that explicit missing-signal modeling contributes to performance under sparse observation conditions.

 5.4 Key Findings summary
 The marginal improvement on UJIIndoorLoc likely reflects dataset-specific characteristics rather than an inherent limitation of the transformer approach. In particular, its strong local spatial structure (Moran’s ) and high sampling density (15.3 samples per location) allow local methods to perform competitively. The dataset’s low SNR (8.2 dB) may further limit the attainable accuracy. By contrast, IndoorCART achieves larger improvements on TUJI1 (15.49%) and the structurally complex SODIndoorLoc dataset (35.12%), suggesting that global attention may be more beneficial under heterogeneous or complex fingerprinting conditions. Overall, the results indicate that hybrid local–global strategies warrant further investigation as a means of balancing performance across diverse environments.
 Section 5.3 and Table 28 provide practical guidance on the dataset conditions under which IndoorCART’s global attention and missing-signal modeling offer the greatest benefits. In this study, the largest gains are associated with limited per-location sampling, relatively high signal-to-noise ratios, and complex multi-building or multi-floor layouts, as represented by TUJI1 and SODIndoorLoc. By contrast, simpler local and ensemble methods remain highly competitive on dense, single-building datasets with strong spatial autocorrelation, such as UJIIndoorLoc, where the transformer’s advantage narrows to a few percent.
 6 Conclusion
 IndoorCART demonstrates the potential of transformer-based coordinate regression for Wi-Fi indoor localization, achieving the lowest error and consistently high values across UJIIndoorLoc, TUJI1, and SODIndoorLoc among the baselines reproduced in this study. However, the magnitude of the improvement varies substantially across datasets. The gain over KNNR on UJIIndoorLoc is marginal and should be interpreted as competitive parity with a slight MAE improvement rather than as decisive evidence that transformers are consistently superior to simpler baselines. Similarly, the TUJI1 MAE of 0.18 m should be interpreted in the context of the fixed dense split used here and not as evidence of strict device-disjoint or location-disjoint generalization.
 The literature-level comparison shows that methods such as L-SwiGLU, HyTra/HyTra-HF, and VITAL report substantially lower raw MAE values; however, these results cannot be treated as controlled head-to-head evidence without identical implementations and evaluation protocols. The extended ablation study further indicates that adaptive masking improves both mean accuracy and tail-error behavior, which may help reduce severe localization failures. Nevertheless, practical deployment requires measured assessments of model size, latency, memory use, and energy consumption, and the present results do not establish complete deployment readiness. Future work should therefore investigate domain adaptation to device and environmental shifts, lightweight transformer designs for real-time inference, multimodal fusion with inertial or magnetic signals, controlled reproduction of recent transformer baselines, deeper dataset-level error analysis, measured computational profiling, leave-device-out and leave-location-out validation, and evaluation across a broader range of real-world indoor environments.
